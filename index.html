The accent, and what a state change is allowed to do

A state change moves lightness and holds hue. A control changes state, not colour. The direction is always away from the ground — lighter on ink, darker on paper — except where the accent is already white, which is the violet ground's whole rule.

ink

hue 288.0°
rest#6C3EE5L 0.523
hover#7449F0L 0.554
press#7C54FAL 0.584

This ground needs two accent values. The brand violet is 3.26:1 here and cannot carry text, but is fine as a fill because its label is white at 6.07:1. So accent #9078FF at 5.95:1 does text, links and focus rings; accent_solid #6C3EE5 does fills.

04

The second voice

Some artwork draws two colours: the accent, and one other. Cyan takes the second part. It sits 74.5° from the accent in OKLCh at half its chroma — far enough to read as its own colour, quiet enough that violet stays the louder of the two.

holds apart

74.5°

accent #9078FF against alt #4BCDE7, drawn at 1px and at 16%.

does not

30.5°

alt #4BCDE7 against teal #5FD2C2. At these weights the pair reads as one colour, which is the defect this role exists to prevent.

Light

One light, from above, committed to across a surface. A raised object shows a hair of highlight along its top edge where the light catches it, an edge that is a spread-only shadow at low alpha rather than an opaque border, and a stack below that tightens at the contact and softens as it falls away — each layer doubling offset and blur and pulling spread further negative.

The stack is neutral on every ground rather than tinted — a violet-tinted shadow on a violet ground disappears. On violet the highlight and edge step up instead, because white on violet reads at a lower alpha than white on ink.

Drafting — one ink, three weights

A technical drawing is not coloured, it is weighted. Every structural line is the same ink and separates by width, opacity and dash — the argument the violet ground already makes for type, applied to line.

Annotation is the deliberate exception. A dimension line and its text are text_dim, never the accent — a dimension measures the subject, it is not the subject, and giving it the accent spends the accent on a ruler. Engraving is the accent at 0.80, mono 7.5px, tracked 2.2px.

The lit case extends the one-light rule into 3D. Key white at 1.10 from above and right, fill #9078FF — the accent — at 0.35 from below and behind, ambient 0.22. Metalness 0.78, roughness 0.46. The fill being the accent is what makes a rendered part read as Solaros rather than as a generic grey render.

Explode and assemble runs 900ms on the standard ease. Reduced motion settle with no travel.

Atmosphere

Ground, then atmosphere, then content. A stack, bottom first: dust, then grain. What is absent is a decision — no vignette, no wash, no field. Auras and grids were ours, not the brand's, and leaving them out of the stack says so where a comment could not.

INK
soft-light · 0.62
PAPER
multiply · 0.12
VIOLET
soft-light · 0.17

One opacity is not one effect. Soft-light does most of its work at mid luminance and almost none near white, so paper takes multiply instead, and violet — which sits right in soft-light's working range — takes far less than ink. Dust is ink-ground only, seeded so every build is byte-identical, and derived from area: max(120, round(w * h * 260 / (1080 * 1350))). A surface may pin its own count with the reason on record; one override stands today.

Figures

Three parametric technical drawings, shipped as solaros-figures.js. Ported from the Industrial Accents exploration; the drafting weights canon publishes were derived from them, so the port was a rename rather than a rewrite.

Drawings that argue, not decoration — each is a mechanism the product actually has. Every stroke uses the published drafting classes, so a figure is styled by the delivery and is correct on any ground without being redrawn. Drag a slider to explode.

Five identical machined rings

Solaros.rings()

One generator, one axis. The detail is the chamfer, the groove round the wall, index marks on the rim, one locating notch and the part number. Nearer rings occlude what sits behind them, so the stack reads solid.

The dispatcher

Solaros.dispatcher()

EOS Max's one distinctive mechanic, drawn. Six prioritised events arrive on pins of falling length — priority read as length — and dispatch straight to virtual state machines. No task scheduler, no ready-list scan. One event and one machine stay at full stroke, and the path between them holds as the parts separate.

A virtual state machine

Solaros.vsm()

Five states, message-driven, three processes stacked on one MCU.

The same machine, on violet

Solaros.vsm()

Nothing is redrawn. The ground changes and the drafting ink resolves to white, because canon names the ink as a role rather than a colour.

15

Surfaces — the same accents, three media

DERIVED — AWAITING CORRECTION. Everything in this section was worked out from each surface's own mechanics rather than ruled, and is waiting for correction. Each surface states which of its mechanics are verified and which are engineering reasoning that has not been measured against a real render.

The five rules

1
It means something.

A part number is on a part. A tick scale measures a thing that is on the page. If the accent points at nothing it is a sticker, and stickers are what the work must never become.

2
One per screen.

A single accent reads as intent. Three read as a theme. The unit of "one" is different on each surface — see `unit` below.

3
One colour stays one colour.

Violet is the only accent. Amber appears once in a whole artefact, as a warning band, and never as decoration.

4
Mono is the voice.

Every label, ID and readout is JetBrains Mono, uppercase, tracked. The sans face never plays instrument. Sizes differ per surface because reading distance does.

5
No fake data.

Every identifier, hash and count is real or is plainly a placeholder. A barcode is a pattern, never a code that pretends to scan.

The thirteen accents, across the three surfaces

Each cell says what the accent becomes on that surface and how it is overused there. The verdicts that are not yes are the useful part — they are where a surface refuses something that works everywhere else.

01

Numeral as object

CSS

Physical originThe number on a hangar door, a part bin, a ticket. It is the biggest thing because it is what you find things by.

Websiteyes

Section markers, large and at low contrast. One per section, never inside a card.

OVERUSE Two large numerals in one viewport, or a numeral that counts nothing.

Presentationyes

The slide's own number, as the object — set large in a corner, or bled off the edge. It is the strongest cheap way to make a deck feel built rather than typed.

OVERUSE A numeral on a slide that is not part of a run. A single slide has nothing to count.

Videoyes, with care

A chapter or shot number on a title card. It may count up as the transition itself, which is the one place the numeral can move.

OVERUSE A numeral that changes faster than it can be read. Under about a second it is a flicker, not a number.

02

Part and log IDs

CSS

Physical originThe stamp on a component. It says this is a specific thing, and it can be traced.

Websiteyes

Anywhere a real identifier exists — a feature, a run, a hash. Long values truncate.

OVERUSE Invented IDs. An ID on something with no identity in the product.

Presentationyes

The same, at 19px or above. A hash is truncated to eight characters and the full value goes in the notes — nobody reads 64 characters from a slide.

OVERUSE A full hash set at chrome size because it fits. It fits and it cannot be read.

Videoyes, truncated

Eight characters, held still, on screen long enough to read. It earns its place when the point is that the thing is traceable.

OVERUSE A scrolling or typing-on ID. The effect reads as a hacker film and the value cannot be read at any frame.

03

Spec plate

CSS

Physical originThe rating plate riveted to a machine: label, value, nothing else.

Websiteyes

Four facts that want to be read in a second. The hand-off section, a build run, the OS page.

OVERUSE Plates for prose. A plate with a sentence in it is a table wearing a costume.

Presentationyes — the strongest of the thirteen here

A whole slide can be a spec plate, up to about six rows. It is the most honest way a deck states facts, and it needs no illustration.

OVERUSE More than six rows, or two plates on one slide. At that point it is a table and should be a table.

Videoyes, built

One row per beat, arriving in reading order. The build is what gives the viewer the pace they cannot set themselves.

OVERUSE All rows at once. On a page the reader scans; in a shot they cannot, and a full plate flashed up is a wall.

04

Tick scale

CSS

Physical originA ruler on the edge of a drawing. It gives the object a size and the reader a position.

Websiteyes

Beside a figure, marking a real quantity — requirements traced, files emitted, progress through a run. It may follow scroll.

OVERUSE A scale that measures nothing, or one on every card.

Presentationyes, frozen

Held at one value, with that value also stated in words. A reader cannot hover to find out what the mark means.

OVERUSE A scale whose value is only readable from its position. On a projected slide, position is approximate.

Videoyes — better here than anywhere

It animates to its value, and the animation IS the information. A count arriving is more legible in motion than as a number that was always there.

OVERUSE Animating for its own sake, or ending on a value the shot never states.

05

Registration marks

CSS

Physical originPrinter's crosses that align the plates. They say something precise was placed here.

Websiteyes

The corners of a product shot or a placeholder. A replacement for the corner brackets, not an addition to them.

OVERUSE Crosses on text blocks. Crosses and brackets together.

Presentationyes

The corners of the slide's image area — and they double as the visible edge of the safe margin, which is useful on a surface that has one.

OVERUSE Crosses on every slide. They mark a placed image, not a page.

Videoconditional

Only at 2px or heavier. A 1px cross is exactly the high-frequency detail an encoder discards, and it will crawl.

OVERUSE Any 1px cross. Also crosses that move — a registration mark that travels is not registering anything.

06

Construction lines

SVG

Physical originThe engineer's drawing behind the object: datums, arcs, centre lines. The object was designed, not dropped.

Websiteyes

Behind a render or the rings. Drawn in the drafting weights.

OVERUSE Lines behind text. Lines unrelated to the object's geometry.

Presentationyes

Behind the slide's object. Strong here, because a slide is already a composed frame and the lines give it a reason to be composed that way.

OVERUSE Construction lines as a background texture on a text slide.

Videoyes — the best transition available

The lines DRAW IN, then the object appears on them. It shows the object being designed, which is the argument the whole brand makes, in the one medium that can show a sequence.

OVERUSE Drawing in lines that the object then ignores. If the geometry does not match, the sequence is a lie the viewer can see.

07

Leader captions

SVG

Physical originThe callout on an exploded drawing: a line from the part to its name.

Websiteyes

From a part to its name — the exploded rings, a build-output figure naming the requirement above the code it produced.

OVERUSE Leaders to things that already have a label under them.

Presentationyes

The same, and the leaders set the reading order of the slide. A reader follows them in the order they are drawn.

OVERUSE More than about four on a slide. Past that the leaders become the subject.

Videoyes, one at a time

Leaders arriving one per beat is the natural build, and each one buys the viewer the time to read the label at its end.

OVERUSE All leaders at once, or leaders that arrive faster than their labels can be read.

08

Dot grid

CSS

Physical originPerforated metal, a drilled plate, a notebook cover. A surface with a pitch.

Websiteyes

Behind a render or the rings, faded toward one corner. Not on cards with text — that was tried and it fought the grain.

OVERUSE Full-bleed grids. Grids behind reading text.

Presentationyes

Behind a slide's object, faded. Safe here because the surface is fixed and the pitch can be set once.

OVERUSE A grid on a slide that has no object on it.

Videoconditional — 32px pitch or coarser

Only at a coarse pitch. The site's 22px grid is fine high-frequency detail and will alias and crawl through an encode.

OVERUSE The web pitch reused unchanged. It is the single most likely thing on this list to look broken in a finished video.

Rotated edge label

CSS

Physical originThe label on a spine, the number on the flank of a vehicle. Read from the side, because that is where you are.

Websiteyes

The left edge of a large card or figure, once per section at most.

OVERUSE Rotated body copy. More than one rotated label in view.

Presentationyes

The left edge of the slide. It is a good way to carry a section name through a run of slides without spending the headline on it.

OVERUSE A rotated label carrying anything the slide actually needs read. It is orientation, not content.

Only if it holds still for the whole shot. Rotated type that moves cannot be read at any frame.

OVERUSE A rotated label on a moving or transitioning element.

Hazard band

CSS

Physical originThe tape on a step, the stripe on a tail fin. A boundary you must notice.

Websiteyes — once site-wide

The beta mark, or a draft build's state. The one place amber may appear.

OVERUSE Stripes as pattern. Two of them anywhere.

Presentationyes — once per deck

A state slide: draft, not for distribution, pre-production. One deck, one band.

OVERUSE A band on every slide of a draft deck. Once is a warning; every slide is a wallpaper.

Videoyes — once per video

Amber survives an encode well, because the stripe is broad and low-frequency. It is the safest strong mark on this surface.

OVERUSE A band that animates. A hazard stripe is a boundary, and a boundary that moves is not one.

Tear line

CSS

Physical originThe perforation on a ticket. Two parts that were one thing, and can be separated.

Websiteyes

The join between a specification and what was cut from it — the hand-off, the export, the CSV that leaves.

OVERUSE As a section divider. The page has just removed its lines; this would be a fancier line.

Presentationyes

The split between what was specified and what was produced, on one slide. It can also be the slide boundary itself in a two-part build.

OVERUSE A perforation between two things that were never one thing.

Videoyes — as a wipe

The transition tears along the perforation. It is the one transition in this list that means something: the artefact separating into its parts.

OVERUSE Using it as a general-purpose wipe between unrelated shots.

Barcode as pattern

CSS

Physical originThe mark that lets a machine read what a person reads. On a screen it can only be texture, honestly.

Websiteyes, small

A small faded area on an export or package figure. Never with digits under it.

OVERUSE A barcode implying it scans to something. Barcodes on cards.

Presentationyes, small

The same. Small, faded, on a figure about something leaving the system.

OVERUSE A barcode at slide scale. Large, it stops being texture and starts claiming to be a code.

Videono

Nothing. A 1-to-3px repeating vertical pattern is the worst case for an encoder — it is pure high-frequency detail with no motion, and it will smear and pulse.

OVERUSE Any use at all on this surface. If a package needs marking, use the ID instead.

Coordinate readout

CSS

Physical originThe instrument reading: where the thing is right now. Live, tabular, exact.

Websiteyes

Attached to a figure, showing one requirement's real position in the output. It may update as the reader interacts.

OVERUSE Readouts of invented values. The cyber-brutalist coordinate for its own sake.

Presentationyes, as a snapshot

A frozen reading, carrying a run id or a timestamp so it is honestly a snapshot. Nothing is live on a slide, and a readout that implies it is, lies.

OVERUSE A readout marked live, or with a state like Running, on a surface where nothing runs.

Videoyes — the one honest live case

It may tick and update as the shot plays. This is the only surface where a value can change without the viewer acting, so it is the only place `live` is true without interaction.

OVERUSE Values ticking faster than they can be read, or a readout that changes with no cause shown in the shot.
